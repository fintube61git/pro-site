Human Agency, Control, and Possibility

What makes a choice possible in a person's lived experience? My research examines how social and technological environments shape what people can perceive, bear, and act on. It brings psychology, law, and systems thinking to questions about coercive influence, human evaluation, and well-being.

Agency-Space Theory provides the theoretical foundation for my research on coercive influence. My Fielding project builds on that framework by developing and testing a new measure of agency constraint during group membership and after leaving.

Agency-Space Theory: The Theoretical Foundation

Being able to name an option is different from experiencing it as something one can do. Agency-Space Theory proposes an account of how controlling environments can narrow the meanings, relationships, identities, and actions that feel possible to a person.

The preprint distinguishes outward behavior from the conditions that make a choice available. It proposes that constraint may persist after external conditions change. These are theoretical claims to be tested, rather than established findings or a validated assessment.

This framework is the groundwork for the Fielding study below, which develops measurement and tests specific questions arising from AST.

From Control Practices to Agency-Space: Measuring What Controlling Groups Do Inside a Person

I am an Institute for Social Innovation Fellow at Fielding Graduate University. This study builds on Agency-Space Theory by translating its account of internal constraint into survey items and testing what those items can measure.

This project develops and tests a new measure of how membership in a controlling group narrows what a person can see, bear, and act on, and whether that narrowing lasts after they leave. Through an anonymous, preregistered online survey of about 300 former group members, it examines whether the new measure adds anything to existing measures of group conduct and post-exit difficulties.

The proposed study asks whether reported control practices predict constraint during membership; whether constraint persists after exit beyond current pressure and mood; whether the new measure adds information about post-exit difficulties; and whether its proposed facets are distinct.

The survey is planned research. The new measure has not been validated, and no survey findings are reported here.

Demoralization, Community Connectedness, and Well-Being

My published research with Lauren Mizock, James Vivian, Alayne J. Ormerod, and lore m. dickey examines demoralization among transgender and gender diverse individuals: its distinction from depression and its associations with community connectedness and well-being.

This work keeps experiences of distress in view alongside people's relationships with community. The article record and full citation are linked below; the complete publication list includes related work on employment stigma, culture, and mental health.

Tools for Self-Directed Exploration
